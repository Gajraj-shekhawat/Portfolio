RAG Explained: Building a Chatbot over Your Own Documents

A chatbot that answers from your policies, manuals or course notes, and shows where each answer came from. Here's how RAG works and where it goes wrong.

Most businesses don't want a chatbot that knows "everything on the internet". They want one that answers from their documents: policies, product manuals, course material, contracts. The standard way to build that is called RAG, retrieval-augmented generation.

How RAG works, in five steps

  1. Split documents into chunks of a few paragraphs each, keeping headings with the text they belong to.
  2. Turn each chunk into an embedding, a list of numbers that captures its meaning.
  3. Store them in a vector index (for example pgvector in PostgreSQL, or a dedicated vector database).
  4. When a question comes in, embed it and fetch the most similar chunks.
  5. Give those chunks to the language model with the question and an instruction to answer only from them, and to cite them.

Where RAG usually goes wrong

  • Bad chunking: tables split in half, or answers spread across chunks that never get retrieved together.
  • Only vector search: exact terms like product codes and names are better found with keyword search. Combining both (hybrid search) works best.
  • No citations: users can't check answers, so they stop trusting the bot.
  • Stale data: documents change but the index doesn't. Re-index on every update.
  • No "I don't know": the model should say when the documents don't contain the answer.

Measure it

Before launch, write 30–50 real questions with the correct answers and run them every time you change chunking, prompts or models. It's the only reliable way to know whether a change made things better.

When RAG isn't the right tool

If the answers live in a database (orders, students, sales), retrieval over text is the wrong approach; an agent that writes safe SQL is better. That's what I built over 1.7 million school records (how it works).
